# The philosophy

Ten principles, in three groups, from the wiki's `Bogleheads® investment philosophy`
page. What follows is a working summary — fetch the page itself when the question turns
on the details.

## Prepare to invest

**1. Live below your means.** Money left over at month's end is the precondition for
everything else. Budgeting matters here mainly as visibility into income and expenses,
and the wiki draws a hard line between needs and wants.

**2. Develop a workable plan.** Sound financial lifestyle first: a budget covering
necessities, discretionary spending, big-ticket saving, and retirement. Pay off bad debt
first. Then write the plan down — an investment policy statement. The wiki's framing is
that the enemy of a good plan is the search for a perfect one; make assumptions and
revise them as you learn more. Writing it down is what gives you the discipline to hold
during a crash.

**3. Never bear too much or too little risk.** The honest test is "will I sell during
the next bear market?" — hard to answer before you've lived through one. Stocks are
necessary for expected return and are genuinely volatile (down ~50% in 2008). Bonds
dampen that. Balance ability, willingness, and need to take risk.

Rules of thumb, all explicitly labeled crude starting points, not answers:
- Bogle: roughly your age in bonds; variants use age−10 or age−20
- Graham: never less than 25% or more than 75% in stocks
- Presence or absence of a pension changes willingness and need substantially

Bonds are for safety — take risk on the equity side, not the bond side. Prefer bond
funds over individual bonds for diversification. Manage interest-rate risk with
short/intermediate duration, default risk with credit quality.

**4. Invest early and often.** ~20% of income is the baseline savings rate; more if
retiring before 65 or leaving significant assets behind. Automate the deduction. The
wiki is explicit that saving regularly matters more than fund selection when you're
starting out.

## Create a portfolio

**5. Invest with simplicity.** Simplicity is the master key. A single total-market index
fund holds thousands of stocks across all styles and cap sizes. The three-fund portfolio
(US total stock, total international stock, US total bond) is the community default;
some add TIPS as a fourth. If everything is in tax-advantaged space, a single
target-date or LifeStrategy fund is a legitimate complete answer. Simplicity lowers
costs and taxes, simplifies rebalancing and tax prep, and lets heirs take over the
portfolio.

**6. Use index funds when possible.** Lowest-cost way to own the whole market.
International typically runs 20–40% of the equity allocation. Where a 401(k) offers no
index funds, look for the largest, most diversified fund with the lowest expense ratio.

**7. Minimize costs.** The gap between a 0.15% and 1.5% expense ratio compounds to
hundreds of thousands of dollars over an investing lifetime. Roughly, 1% of extra annual
cost costs you about ten years of retirement funding.

**8. Minimize taxes.** Asset allocation comes first — it's the single most influential
decision — and taxes are considered only after the total portfolio is configured. Fill
tax-advantaged accounts first. If you also hold taxable: total-market equity index funds
are tax-efficient (low dividends and capital gains); bond funds are tax-inefficient
(interest taxed at your full marginal rate), as are REITs, small-value, and high-turnover
active funds — those belong in tax-advantaged space. If bonds won't fit and you're in a
high bracket, municipal bond funds in taxable are reasonable. Tax-loss harvesting turns
downturns into immediate tax savings.

## Maintain discipline

**9. Never try to time the market.** Typical fund investors underperform the very funds
they hold, because they buy after good performance and sell after bad. Research shows no
evidence of positive timing skill. Buy high, sell low is the reliable outcome.

**10. Stay the course.** The hardest one. Volatility is the price of equity returns.
Distractions are constant — hot new asset classes, alternatives, fancy vehicles. Set the
allocation, include enough bonds that you can tolerate the drops, rebalance about once a
year, and otherwise ignore financial news.

## The compressed version

A Boglehead investor: saves a lot, picks a stock/bond allocation, buys low-cost broadly
diversified funds, places them tax-efficiently, and stays the course. Setup takes part
of a day; maintenance is about an hour a year.

## Where Bogleheads legitimately disagree

Presenting these as settled would misrepresent the community. When a question lands
here, say that reasonable Bogleheads differ:

- How much international equity (0% to market-weight ~40%)
- Whether to count Social Security and pensions as bond-equivalent in the allocation
- Small-cap value and other factor tilts vs pure total market
- TIPS vs nominal bonds, and duration choices
- Whether to hold any REITs separately
- Paying off a low-rate mortgage early vs investing the difference
- Traditional vs Roth at middle income levels

## Premises the framework rejects

Say so directly, explain once, don't lecture:

- Picking individual stocks to beat the market
- Timing entry or exit based on forecasts or valuations
- Chasing recent top performers
- Actively managed funds where an index alternative exists
- Paying AUM fees for portfolio management that a three-fund portfolio replicates
- Leveraged or inverse ETFs as long-term holdings
- Treating crypto or gold as a core allocation
